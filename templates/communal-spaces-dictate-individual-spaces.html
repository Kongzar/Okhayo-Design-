Communal Spaces Dictate Individual Spaces

Published September 13, 2026

Origins & Community Before a single dome-shaped home was raised, before a hearth was ever dug into the earth, the homestead began with a circle. Empty. Marked out - left open, like a held breath. And everything that followed — every sleeping space, every doorway, every threshold a family would ever cross in this life — would be built in relation to that circle. Not beside it. Not near it. In relation to it. The kraal came first. Before a single dome-shaped home was raised, before a hearth was ever dug into the earth, the homestead began with a circle. Empty. Marked out - left open, like a held breath. And everything that followed — every sleeping space, every doorway, every threshold a family would ever cross in this life — would be built in relation to that circle. Not beside it. Not near it. In relation to it. Let that rest with you a moment. This is no small stone dropped in still water. This is the river itself. The Western mind tends to build a settlement from the private outward — the home first, the personal space, raised in solitude, and the public spaces stitched on afterward like an afterthought. A space for children here, if there's room. A common area there, if it's convenient. But that was never our way. Our ancestors began with the collective, and let the individual answer to it. The Isibaya, the Umzi — these were never additions to the homestead. The homestead was the thing arranged around them. Perhaps you're thinking: but couldn't this simply be about the cattle? About keeping the herd close, safe, seen? I wondered the same, once. But the placing of things runs deeper than any fence built to hold in a cow. The great house — indlu enkulu, the most honoured dwelling in the whole homestead — does not face a road. It does not turn toward a pleasant view, or lean into the sunrise, or angle itself for anyone's convenience. It faces the kraal. Every time, without exception. The most important private space in the entire homestead bows itself toward the communal one, and never the other way around. And it is there, in that same kraal-facing room, that the ceremonies are held, the ancestors are spoken to, and the deepest moments of a family's life unfold — the births, the deaths, the disputes, the celebrations. Take the kraal away, and the whole spatial poem of the homestead falls apart. Nothing else remembers where it was meant to stand

That is the moment it became clear to me. This was never only architecture. This was a spiritual instruction, written into the land itself — and we have been walking past it for generations, never once stopping to ask what it was trying to say to us. Here, then, is what I believe it was saying. There is a deep and living thread between how an African understood themselves as a conscious being upon this earth, and how they folded themselves — body and spirit both — into the land they called home. I believe the circle of our living arrangements was never only about the shape of the hills or the need for safety, though both of those mattered. I believe we understood, in a way our modern minds have half-forgotten, that energy travels more easily in a circle, or better yet, a spiral. That the nearer we stood to one another in body, the stronger the bond grew, and the more fortitude the whole community carried. We began with an understanding of ourselves and our place in the universe — and that understanding spilled outward, gently, into the very structures we raised to shelter us. Our individual homes sheltered us. But it was the communal spaces — the kraal, the fire pit, the gathering ground — that gave us communion. A quiet architecture, confirming without a single word that we were built to function as one. That every member of the homestead was a necessary thread in the larger weave. We ate together. We celebrated together. We judged, we punished, we mourned, and we welcomed new life together — and the very shape of the homestead spoke this truth long before anyone needed to say it aloud

Spaces that prove the argument

Umzi + Isibayo — the isiZulu and Xhosa homestead and cattle kraal: The isibaya was never simply a place to keep livestock. It was the spiritual and social heart of the homestead, and every structure took its bearing from it. — The great house, indlu enkulu, faces the kraal. Its direction is set by the communal space, not the road, not the view. — It is here that the ancestors are addressed, the ceremonies held, the family's most sacred hours lived out. — Remove the kraal, and the whole spatial logic collapses. The ritual space at the centre is the compass point for every sleeping space around it. Kgotla — the Tswana community court: The open-air gathering place at the heart of every traditional Tswana village. Circular. Covered, yet open on every side. The place where a community governs itself. — Every homestead in the village takes its position in relation to the kgotla — the closer a family stands to it, the higher its standing. — The individual dwelling cannot be understood at all without the kgotla at its centre. — Disputes are settled here. Decisions are made here. The identity of the whole community is performed here, in the open, for all to witness. I believe we planned the inner rooms of the homestead first, and let everything else grow outward from there. At the centre: the kraal, holding the homestead's wealth and its roaming ground, its stores of meat and grain, its sacred earth for the chiefs, its council space — all of it fenced, beautifully, with branches pulled from the very trees around it. A boundary built from the same land it was protecting. Only past those branches would you find the dome-shaped homes themselves. And even these were never shaped by accident. Low entrances, bending the body into humility before anyone dared step into a space so sacred to those who lived there. A fire pit at the centre, for warmth, for food, for the quiet work of drawing near to one's ancestors. And notice this — most of life was never lived inside that private room at all. The days were spent outside it, immersed in the land, roaming, labouring alongside the rest of the homestead. That is why the individual space stayed so small, so spare. It was never built to hold a whole life. It was built to let you rest, before you returned to the part of life that actually mattered — the shared part. Now look at what we have done with that order. We have turned it inside out. We have poured our comfort, our money, our very sense of self into the private space — the walled bedroom, the gated property, the guarded estate — and let the communal spaces wither down to almost nothing. We gather now for a drink, or for work, inside cold and unnatural rooms, like people who were never born of this land at all. We no longer fuel one another. We drain each other, and then retreat behind our own walls to recover from the draining — which is exactly the reverse of how we were ever meant to fill ourselves back up. I believe we need to return real focus, real resources, real reverence to our public spaces. Urban planning should treat parks, resting places, reading corners, and gathering grounds as essential — not as decoration, but as the very spaces that honour every person in a community equally, and remind us of our closeness to nature rather than our distance from it. I understand why this sounds naive to some ears. Our cities have changed, and safety has, understandably, become the priority. But the same energy we now pour into securing our private homes could be poured, instead, into securing what we share — and it would do something no higher wall ever could. It would force us to hold one another accountable again, out in the open. Not behind a screen. In public. This is my true worry with the gated estates creeping along the edges of our cities. We are sensing, correctly, a hunger — for community, for people who share our values, for the strength that comes from living close together. But we are chasing that hunger with fences and gates — fearful tools that only deepen the very isolation we are trying to outrun. So here is where I land. Communal space was never only a design choice made for "the survival of the tribe." It was a spiritual understanding — a recognition of our bond not only to one another, but to the earth beneath us. Energy moved between us and through us, and merged us with the land around us. We did not simply survive that way. We thrived. Together. The Audacious African
